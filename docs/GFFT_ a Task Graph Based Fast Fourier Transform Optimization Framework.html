Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is a widely used mathematical tool in scientific and engineering applications, and optimizing its performance remains a challenging problem. This paper introduces GFFT, a novel task-graph-based FFT optimization framework that leverages modern hardware and software techniques to achieve high-performance computation. GFFT features a tuning model that uses hardware parameters to optimize FFT decomposition, a bi-directional recursive FFT algorithm that avoids strided load in SIMD implementation, and several graph optimizers inspired by deep learning frameworks to enhance performance. In addition, GFFT utilizes task-based parallelism to exploit performance on multi-core processors and provide potential compatibility with heterogeneous systems. Experimental results demonstrate that GFFT outperforms popular FFT frameworks, achieving an average speedup of 1.17x to FFTW and 1.27x to oneMKL on the Intel Xeon processor, 1.18x to AOCL-FFTW on the AMD EPYC processor, and 2.11x to FFTW on the Sunway multi-core processor with a single thread. Additionally, GFFT achieves an average speedup of 11.48x to FFTW and 1.41x to oneMKL on the Intel Xeon processor, 9.87x to AOCL-FFTW on the AMD EPYC processor with 16-threads.

1 INTRODUCTION

Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is a fundamental and widely used mathematical tool in various scientific and engineering applications, including signal processing, image analysis, and computational physics. However, optimizing the performance of FFT computation is an ongoing challenge, especially with the increasing demand for high-performance computing in scientific and industrial domains. The performance of FFT depends on various factors, such as the shape of input data, the choice of the FFT algorithm, and the architecture of hardware.

To achieve high-performance FFT computation, hardware vendors and researchers have developed various FFT frameworks and libraries. However, the existing FFT frameworks mainly focus on optimizing the computation kernels, they do not pay much attention to the workflow of the execution plan, which has been shown to significantly impact the performance. They are also facing several challenges, including the need for manual tuning and optimization, the limitations of algorithmic choices and parallelization strategies, and the sub-optimal use of hardware resources.

In this paper, we introduce GFFT, a task-graph-based FFT framework to overcome these challenges and achieve high-performance FFT computation. GFFT is designed to be flexible and efficient, and it offers several features from a modern perspective of FFT optimization and supports power-of-two complex FFT with single and double precision. To enable better performance than existing frameworks, GFFT builds upon the existing FFT computation kernels as building blocks and utilizes a tuning model and optimization techniques to determine a near-optimal blueprint for building a high-performance FFT execution plan. GFFT's tuning model uses measured hardware parameters and integer linear programming (ILP) to optimize FFT decomposition, and its task graph provides representations of complex FFT algorithms. Furthermore, GFFT employs strategies of parallelization to exploit performance with near-optimal multi-thread execution.

In the following sections, we describe the design and implementation of GFFT, present experimental results to evaluate its performance on different platforms, and discuss its strengths, limitations, and potential applications. We believe that GFFT offers a promising solution to the challenging problem of FFT optimization and can benefit a wide range of scientific and engineering applications that rely on FFT computation.

2 BACKGROUND

Optimizing the performance of FFT computation has been a subject of research for several decades, and numerous approaches have been proposed to address this problem. This section reviews the most relevant and recent works on FFT optimization, including FFT algorithms, and libraries.

2.1 FFT Algorithms

The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) converses sequences between the time and frequency domains by discrete sampling. A DFT transforms a sequence x of N complex numbers into another sequence X of complex numbers with the following equation:

Xk=N∑m=0xmωmkNXk=∑m=0NxmωNmk
(1)
where 0 ≤ k < N and ωN=e−2πNiωN=e−2πNi.

The FFT is a more efficient algorithm for computing the DFT by exploiting the symmetry properties and using a divide-and-conquer strategy to reduce the time complexity. The most commonly used FFT algorithm is the Cooley-Tukey algorithm [11], which recursively decomposes the input sequence into smaller sub-sequences and combines their DFTs to obtain the final result. When the size of input sequence N satisfies N = r1r2, the equation 1 can be written as:

Xk2r1+k1=r2−1∑m2=0[ωm1k1n(r1−1∑m1=0xm1r2+m2ωm1k1r1)]ωm2k2r2,Xk2r1+k1=∑m2=0r2−1[ωnm1k1(∑m1=0r1−1xm1r2+m2ωr1m1k1)]ωr2m2k2,
(2)
In the FFT equation, the output sequence X has a size of N and is indexed by k = k2r1 + k1 where 0 ≤ k1 < r1 and 0 ≤ k2 < r2, the input sequence x is also given. r1, r2 are the radix factors that decompose the input sequence. To form the output sequence by index, the algorithm employs two nested loops: The inner k1-loop performs a DFT of radix-r1, while the outer k2-loop performs DFTs of radix-r2 on the result of the previous DFT transform, with scaling by twiddle factors. Hence, the original DFT of size N has been decomposed as multiple DFTs of radix-r1 and multiple DFTs of radix-r2. With further recursive decomposition, Cooley-Tukey FFT can reduce the computational complexity to O(nlog n). However, implementing this algorithm leads to multiple bit-reversal permutations during calculations, which can be less cache-friendly on modern computer architectures.

Stockham's autosort FFT algorithm inserts out-of-place transposition between each radix calculation to benefit from cache hierarchy and extension of Single Instruction Multiple Data (SIMD) [28, 29]. This modification greatly improves the performance of the FFT computation and makes it a practical tool for various scientific and engineering applications, providing the basis for large-scale parallel DFT computation [30]. Prime-factor FFT algorithm transforms an FFT of size N with prime factors N = N1N2 into a 2D-FFT of size N1 × N2 by re-indexing of elements [19]. Rader's FFT algorithm rewrites a DFT of prime size N as a cyclic convolution of size N − 1 to make the problem solvable by FFT or convolution [27]. Bluestein's algorithm is another widely used FFT algorithm for computing DFTs of arbitrary size. It rewrites a DFT of arbitrary size N as a pair of cyclic convolutions, with zero padding to be solved by a more efficient FFT algorithm [8]. The main advantage of Bluestein's algorithm is its flexibility to compute DFTs of arbitrary size, making it a popular choice in FFT libraries as a generic and efficient solution for FFT computation.

The FFT algorithm is primarily designed using a divide-and-conquer strategy, which enables the combination of the aforementioned algorithms to solve larger FFT problems.

2.2 FFT Libraries

There exists a variety of FFT libraries that are designed for different architectures, including CPU, GPU, and other co-processors. Hardware vendors have provided widely used FFT libraries on their platforms, such as oneMKL [31], AMD Optimizing CPU Libraries (AOCL) [1], ARM Performance Library (ARMPL) [5], and Apple vDSP [4]. Those vendor-built libraries exploit the performance of processors with SIMD extensions and other hardware features. Other vendor-built libraries like NVIDIA cuFFT [25], AMD clFFT [2], and rocFFT [3] provide FFT computations on GPU platforms through CUDA, OpenCL, and HIP. These libraries support the execution of multiple transforms simultaneously to take advantage of the parallelism and memory bandwidth of the GPU.

While most vendor-built libraries are not open-source, several open-source and efficient CPU-based FFT libraries are available. The Fastest Fourier Transform in the West (FFTW) is one of the most popular libraries that provides FFT computations on each CPU architecture, including x86, ARM, MIPS, and RISC-V [14, 15, 16, 18]. FFTW utilizes code generation (GENFFT) to generate a range of optimized kernels that compute FFT with radix 2/3/5/7/11 and other non-primes radices like 4/6/8/16 [18]. FFTW uses a SIMD template embedded in generated kernels to adopt SIMD extensions on different platforms. In FFTW, the FFT computation can be decomposed into two types of kernels: direct and twiddle. The direct kernels perform FFT of the innermost radix, while the twiddle kernels multiply input with twiddle factors and perform FFT of other radices. By manipulating the input and output stride, those kernels can be combined into all kinds of FFT algorithms and described as the execution plan. Different from Stockham's autosort FFT algorithm, FFTW performs the out-of-place bit-reversal permutation at the leaves(direct kernels) of the recursion, which implicitly improves the data locality [9, 16]. Thus, the plan in FFTW forms a tree whose leaf nodes are the out-of-place direct kernels and other nodes are the in-place twiddle kernels. The difference in the composition of the tree may significantly affect the performance, resulting in a different number of arithmetic operations and memory access patterns. FFTW utilizes auto-tuning to select the fastest plan among various FFT algorithms and different factorizations. The most efficient plan for 8192-point FFT in FFTW is illustrated in Fig. 1.

AutoFFT [23] is a highly optimized FFT framework, which utilizes various ISA-based optimization techniques in its assembly generator to generate highly optimized FFT kernels for x86 and ARM processors. Similar to FFTW, it adopts auto-tuning to determine the optimal execution plan. In addition, its butterfly network is optimized using several techniques, including rearranging the output of the innermost radix in Stockham's algorithm, applying SIMD across outer radices, and combining the breath-first and depth-first orders in execution. However, it ignores the potential parallelism of FFT kernels and lost the flexibility of the framework. UHFFT [24] uses a similar auto-tuning method and generates ISA-specific code to improve the performance. Spiral re-expresses the FFT formula in the Signal Processing Language. It forms an optimization problem to exploit the optimal mathematical structure on a given target architecture by considering a wide range of factors that affect the performance and generate the optimized source code of the FFT problem [13, 26]. FFTS [7] adopts dynamic code generation and other optimizations to provide high-performance computing on a wide range of x86 and ARM processors.

The existing FFT libraries mainly focus on optimizing the computation kernels of the FFT algorithm using code generation and hand-written methods. These kernels work like building blocks and can achieve high performance by taking into account hardware parameters. With auto-tuning techniques and dynamic programming, the libraries can find a near-optimal execution plan for the FFT computation of any size.

However, existing FFT libraries suffer from several issues. Firstly, they do not take into account the execution plan and parallelization strategies, which can significantly impact performance, as demonstrated in FFTW and AutoFFT. Secondly, the SIMD dimension in the first radix calculations of FFTW and AutoFFT is usually not continuous, resulting in high latency of SIMD load/store operations. Thirdly, auto-tuning can be costly for processors with low memory bandwidth. For example, on Sunway multi-core processors, FFTW takes minutes to find the tuned plan, which is not acceptable for most applications. Lastly, the blueprint for decomposing the FFT computation in these libraries is often similar, limiting the search space during auto-tuning and resulting in locally optimal solutions. These issues highlight the need for an improved FFT library.

3 THE GFFT FRAMEWORK

In this section, we present the three fundamental components of the GFFT framework: the task graph, operations, and the tuning model illustrated in Fig. 2. The task graph serves as the intermediate representation (IR) of the FFT plan, while the operations represent the generated kernels that execute portions of the FFT computations. The tuning model selects the near-optimal FFT plan based on the FFT problem size and hardware parameters.

3.1 Task Graph

Existing FFT frameworks represent execution plans using loops and recursive functions [7, 14, 15, 16, 18, 23], which can become inefficient and difficult to manage as the FFT plan becomes more complex and data dependencies between kernels increase.

To address this issue, we propose a task-graph-based IR, which provides a more efficient and flexible approach to representing FFT computations. By defining the FFT operations as tasks, we can create a directed acyclic graph that neatly represents arithmetic operations and their dependencies. This approach has shown great potential for optimization and parallelization and has been proven to be efficient in various fields such as rendering engines and deep-learning inference frameworks [21, 32].

We adopt the task graph to organize the FFT decomposition operations. The vertex set V of the task graph consists of the FFT operations C and memory operations M, that is, V = {v∣v ∈ C ∪ M}. To illustrate this, Fig. 3 presents the representation of the FFT plan with a size of 8192 corresponding to Fig. 1, where arrows indicate the read-after-write (RAW) dependency. Direct and twiddle operations with various radices are inserted corresponding to the two types of kernels in FFTW. Memory operations such as transpose are inserted to implement the out-of-place bit-reversal permutation. Each operation requires several parameters, including v_loopv_loop to represent the number of iterations, tw to represent the twiddle factors that the operation needs, and input and output descriptors. All parameters of the operations are precompiled and saved on each node.

Compared to FFTW and other frameworks, which employ a tree to search and execute plans recursively, the task graph approach provides a simpler and more efficient way to schedule and execute tasks, as the task dependency has been embedded in the graph. And the run-time overhead can be reduced by storing precompiled parameters. Additionally, independent sub-graphs can be dispatched and executed in a multi-core or heterogeneous environment, exploiting parallelism to improve performance.

3.2 Operations

Our operation definitions are highly compatible with most of the existing FFT frameworks, including FFTW, Spiral, and AutoFFT. We integrate the FFT operations of FFTW into our framework of its open-source nature and high performance. Therefore we can leverage its existing work to enhance the code generator. As a result, we add new features to automatically generate FFT kernels, including:

  • Fine-tuning the parameters of the generator;
  • Implementing a SIMD template with additional intrinsics, such as in-register transposition;
  • Adding support for generating fused kernels;
  • Providing language support for integrating kernels.

We generate two types of FFT operations with SIMD extensions, which we refer to as direct and twiddle operations based on their definitions in FFTW. Algorithm 1 and Algorithm 2 provide the definitions, where an out-of-place FFT computation is performed on input X and stored in output Y, with the same memory layout, as some code generators do not support generating kernels with in-place calculations. Each kernel is generated with a C++ template wrapper that supports interleaved complex input and output format with a radix from 2 to 64.

To further optimize the calculation, we generate specialized kernels that are tailored to the characteristics of the input and output data. For instance, optimized kernels that only handle sequences contiguous in memory are generated to provide additional optimization. Our framework also includes implementations of memory operations, such as transpose (illustrated in Algorithm 3), strided memory copy, and barrier.

3.3 Tuning Model

Auto-tuning is a commonly used method for optimizing the FFT decomposition plan in existing FFT frameworks. However, this method becomes more costly for large problems, particularly on CPUs with low memory bandwidth, and may be impacted by unpredictable factors such as cache and TLB misses.

We propose a performance model to guide the search process and determine the near-optimal decomposition of specific FFT problems, alleviating the need for exhaustive searching. Our model considers the cost of direct and twiddle operations within the level-2 cache, which is defined as:

θD={CD2i∣1≤i≤6},θT={CT2i∣1≤i≤6}.θD={CD2i∣1≤i≤6},θT={CT2i∣1≤i≤6}.
(3)
where CD2iCD2i represents the computational cost of the direct operation of size 2i (up to 64), and CT2iCT2i represents the cost of the twiddle operation of size 2i. These costs can be measured at compile-time, allowing us to model the performance of a given decomposition in terms of its actual cost.

Let EN be the estimated computation cost of an FFT problem of size N, and let the decomposed radix of this FFT problem be N = r1 × r2 × … × rp. As FFT problems within the level-2 cache can be computed efficiently on 2 radices (64 × 64 = 4096, two FFT kernels), we can estimate their cost as follows:

Er1r2=r2CDr1+r1CTr2Er1r2=r2CDr1+r1CTr2
(4)
where the radix-r1 direct kernel is executed for r2 times, while the radix-r2 twiddle kernel is executed for r1 times to calculate on entire sequence.

For large FFT problems, FFT operations with long strides can cause data exchange with the level-3 cache or memory. However, as modern Non-Uniform Memory Access (NUMA) architectures typically share the level-3 cache among multiple cores, these interleaved exchanges can be prolonged by the cache coherence protocol. To accurately model this performance, we define the cost of twiddle operations that exchange data with the level-3 cache and memory as CM for the cost of radix from r3 to rp, which can be measured at compile-time similarly:

θM={CM2i∣1≤i≤6}.θM={CM2i∣1≤i≤6}.
(5)

4 OPTIMIZATION

4.1 Bi-directional Recursive FFT Algorithm

Existing FFT frameworks, such as FFTW and AutoFFT, improve performance by implicitly transposing the output of the first radix calculation to avoid bit-reversal permutation. This allows the remaining FFTs to be performed in-place and in column-major order and efficiently incorporated with SIMD extensions. Fig. 4 illustrates the butterfly diagram of this process.

In FFTW, the first radix (marked by the red line) combines two radix-2 FFTs and vectorizes them. This is possible because their outputs are stored contiguously in memory, as shown by the blue line, to avoid bit-reversal permutation in the next radix. While the input is not vectorized efficiently due to the non-continuous input across radix calculations. This results in strided data loads, which requires additional SIMD instructions such as insert or shuffle, leading to a significant increase in the latency of memory access and causing the first radix calculation to be slower than the other radix calculations. AutoFFT proposed an optimization by vectorizing across the "stages", which are all the first radix calculations. While along with its cache-friendly execution order, AutoFFT vectorizes and executes the first radix calculations just like FFTW.

We discover that vectorizing across all the radix-r1 butterflies without adhering to the cache-friendly execution order can result in the possibility of making the input data continuous, as shown in Fig. 5. Specifically, by vectorizing x0 and x1, as well as x4 and x5, we are able to achieve this outcome, which significantly reduces the latency of the SIMD load. The output stride of the vectorized radix-2 butterfly remains the same but is now more tightly packed between each butterfly, which may improve data locality. Another optimization involves interleaving the output of each butterfly using a shuffle instruction. This process can pack two strided outputs as two continuous outputs, marked as black and red dots in Fig. 5, where data of the same color is calculated within the same SIMD register. FFTW's code generator already supports this process.

Generally, if the decomposition contains more than two radices, the first radix can be vectorized from another direction which is continuous in memory storage. Fig. 6 illustrates this optimization on FFT with N = r1 × r2 × r3, showing that the optimized method vectorizes the radix-r1 calculations along the direction of continuous input, leading to better data locality and reduced latency of SIMD load while avoiding bit-reversal permutation.

To achieve this, we propose an algorithm that guides the decomposition of direct and twiddle operations from two directions. Direct operations are decomposed from bottom to top, while twiddle operations are decomposed from top to bottom, marked red in algorithm 4 and algorithm 5 .

Algorithm 4 describes how the direct operations are executed with the resulting decomposition N = r1 × r2 × r3 × … × rp − 1 × rp. As mentioned in Section 3.2, some code generators do not support generating kernels with varying input and output strides. To address this, we employ explicit transposition to maintain compatibility and avoid the bit-reversal permutation. This approach allows for further optimization, which will be introduced in Section 4.2.

Algorithm 5 outlines the execution of twiddle operations with remaining radices from r2 to rp, whose inner_loopinner_loop is prod of the radices that have been decomposed previously.

To maintain the top-to-bottom data dependency in the task graph for twiddle operations, we decompose direct and twiddle operations in opposite directions of recursion. In each iteration of recursion in the decomposition, a node is created in the task graph, representing the sub-problem that needs to be solved, and it is processed and substituted by its solution expressed as a sub-graph. To maintain data dependency between direct and twiddle operations that are decomposed from opposite directions, a barrier operation is required. Figure 7 illustrates the task graph after processing the algorithm-guided FFT decomposition with N = r1 × r2 × r3. The computations are divided into two phases, the reverse recursion of the direct operation phase and the recursion of the twiddle operation phase.

The bi-directional recursion can significantly improve the performance of direct operations on large FFT where the decomposition holds more than two radices, and our task graph can further optimize and execute this plan with minimal overhead.

4.2 Graph Optimization

We develop several graph optimizers to enhance the performance of our FFT framework. These optimizers are inspired by the optimization techniques used in deep learning frameworks [10, 20], and are designed according to capture the particular data dependency of FFT.

Graph Substitution. The FFT sub-problem is represented as an unresolved node in the task graph, and our GFFT framework uses graph-substitution optimizer to recursively solve each sub-problem guided by the FFT algorithm and tuning model. We implement several optimizers to decompose FFT sequence and enable high-performance FFT computation, including an optimizer based on the bi-directional recursive FFT algorithm, an optimizer based on FFTW's leave-transposition FFT algorithm [16], and an optimizer for multi-threaded FFT execution.

Operation Fusion. To improve the efficiency of FFT execution, we develops several optimizers based on kernel fusion. These optimizations aim to eliminate the data exchange of intermediate results by fusing operations into a single kernel. Fig. 8 illustrates examples of operation fusion.

One approach is to fuse the direct operation and its following transpose operation into a single kernel, which is possible when the direct kernels support varying input and output strides. Another approach is to fuse the direct operation and its following twiddle operation into a single kernel, which can efficiently utilize SIMD extension and reduce the data exchange of intermediate results. As the direct kernels apply SIMD on multiple batches of FFT, there has to be more than one radix to efficiently utilize SIMD extension. This involves additional memory access between the direct kernel and the following twiddle kernel, which needs to write intermediate results to memory. However, if the intermediate results and implicit transposition can be saved and performed on registers (depending on the number of registers and size of FFT), the direct kernel and its following twiddle kernel can be fused into one kernel, which is described in Section 3.2.

Constant Folding. We develops an optimizer to pre-compute and pack the twiddle factors based on the decomposition and generated kernels, which reduces calculation in runtime and improves the performance. We adopt the twiddle factor calculation scheme based on the generated kernels, which is a commonly used approach in existing FFT frameworks. Furthermore, we improve data locality by allowing twiddle operations that hold the same parameters to share their twiddle factors. This optimization reduces the memory footprint and improves the data locality.

4.3 Parallel Execution

As mentioned in Section 4.2, we develop an optimizer for multi-threading FFT execution to exploit the parallelism of multi-core processors. By decomposing the FFT problem into several independent sub-graphs, it can be executed using multi-threads:

We first select the number of threads, denoted as nthr, based on the size of the input array N and the maximum size M within the capacity of the level-2 cache as NUMA shares level-3 caches on dies. Meanwhile, nthr is selected such that it does not exceed the configured number of threads config_nthrconfig_nthr.

nthr=min(config_nthr,max(⌊N/M⌋,1))nthr=min(config_nthr,max(⌊N/M⌋,1))
(10)
Hence, a conservative number of threads is chosen to avoid thread startup overhead and cache consistency issues when the input size N is too small.

Next, we set nthr as a fixed radix in the decomposition, with the remaining size NnthrNnthr fed into the tuning model to obtain the decomposition of the sub-problem on each thread.

Last, we apply the multi-thread optimizer to the task graph, generating an execution plan guided by the decomposition and FFT algorithm. We do not utilize our bi-directional recursive FFT algorithm here, as it may introduce cache consistency issues between independent sub-graphs. Additionally, the last twiddle operation on radix-nthr is split into nthr parts to fully exploit parallelism. To ensure correctness, barrier operations are inserted before and after this sub-graph.

Two phases of FFT parallel executions are created to fully utilize the multi-core processor, as illustrated in Fig. 9. By topologically sorting the task graph, each sub-graph will be scheduled as a task queue and dispatched to the multi-threading environment. We use the depth-first recursive method for topological sorting, which has been shown to offer advantages in cache utilization [6, 7, 23]. Multi-threading is implemented using OpenMP [12]. Moreover, the task-based parallelism allows for the mapping of sub-graphs onto other hardware, enabling support for heterogeneous architectures such as NVIDIA GPU and Sunway-26010.

5 EVALUATION

In this section, we first evaluate the performance of GFFT on Intel, AMD, and Sunway multi-core processors, and compare it with FFTW and oneMKL on the Intel platform, FFTW and AMD AOCL-FFTW on the AMD platform, FFTW on the Sunway platform. The performance of multi-threaded FFT computations is evaluated on each platform with OpenMP enabled. Then, the FFT plan and setup time are evaluated on Intel and Sunway platforms. Last, we analyze the effectiveness of the optimization techniques employed in GFFT.

For evaluation, we use FFTW 3.3.10, oneMKL 2022.0.2, and AOCL-FFTW 3.1.0, compiled with SIMD enabled. FFTW is compiled with SIMD extension and evaluated with FFTW_MEASURE mode. Specifically, FFTW enables AVX2 extension on the Intel and the AMD platform, generic256 (FFTW-GEN256) and customized (FFTW-SWSIMD) extension on the Sunway platform. The generic256 extension in FFTW involves automatic vectorization by the compiler. FFTW does not support SW-SIMD, so we integrate a customized extension plugin to utilize it. To ensure compatibility with the evaluation programs, GFFT implements the interface of both FFTW and oneMKL and builds with AVX2 on the Intel and the AMD platform, with SW-SIMD on the Sunway platform. Our tuning model automatically adapts to each platform at compile-time. Details of the platforms and evaluation settings are provided in Table 1.

5.1 Performance

The performance is measured using Giga Floating-point Operations Per Second (GFLOPS), a common metric used in evaluations of FFT frameworks [14, 17, 23]:

GFLOPS=10−9⋅5N⋅log2NTGFLOPS=10−9⋅5N⋅log2⁡NT
(11)
where N denotes the size of the FFT problem, and T denotes the time of execution in seconds.

The performance comparison of GFFT, FFTW, and oneMKL on the Intel Xeon processor with double complex is presented in Fig. 10. For the single-threaded FFT computation, GFFT achieves an average speedup of 1.17x and 1.27x over FFTW and oneMKL, respectively, and a maximum of 1.66x and 3.46x over the two libraries. With 4, 8, and 16 threads, GFFT shows an average speedup of 12.57x and 1.52x over FFTW and oneMKL, 11.97x, 1.40x, 11.48x, and 1.41x, respectively. GFFT, FFTW, and oneMKL exhibit similar performance trends in single-threaded FFT computations (Fig. 10 (a)), and there are two drops in performance trends at problem sizes of 1024 and 32768, corresponding to the capacity of level-1 and level-2 cache. Our bi-directional recursive FFT algorithm shows significant improvement over FFTW for large problem sizes, while the performance of FFTW drops rapidly as the problem size increases. In multi-threaded calculations (illustrated in Fig. 10 (b), (c), (d)), our parallelization strategy for the number of threads shows significant improvement compared to FFTW for problem sizes smaller than 2048. Furthermore, the performance curves suggest that oneMKL may adopt a similar strategy to avoid performance issues.

The performance of GFFT, FFTW, and AOCL-FFTW on the AMD EPYC processor with double complex is demonstrated in Fig. 11. GFFT outperforms FFTW and AOCL-FFTW in most cases, achieving up to 1.19x and 1.18x faster speed in single-thread FFT computations, respectively. Furthermore, GFFT also shows remarkable improvement in multi-threading calculations, with an average speedup of 44.73x and 8.66x over FFTW and AOCL-FFTW with 4 threads, 44.53x and 9.30x with 8 threads, 45.78x and 9.87x with 16 threads. The performance curves in Fig. 11 reveal that all algorithms suffered from performance drops when the problem size exceeded the capacity of the level-3 cache. GFFT's superiority in utilizing 16 threads is also evident, while FFTW and AOCL-FFTW are misled by the auto-tuning in multi-threaded executions.

The performance comparison of GFFT, FFTW-GEN256, and FFTW-SWSIMD on the Sunway 3231 processor with double complex is presented in Fig. 12. GFFT significantly outperforms FFTW-GEN256 and FFTW-SWSIMD in single-thread FFT, achieving an average of 1.26x and 2.11x speedup, respectively. In multi-threading calculations, GFFT consistently shows superior performance across all thread counts. The improvement in performance occasionally declined as the size increased to 131072, at which point FFTW began adopt plans via R2C FFT to optimize memory access. Similar circumstances are observed in both Intel and AMD platforms. Our subsequent research will focus on incorporating real number FFT support and associated algorithms.

Table 2 presents a comparison of the FFT plan and performance (in GFLOPS) between GFFT and FFTW on the Intel Xeon processor. Our tuning model outperforms FFTW's auto-tuning in most cases, regardless of whether our optimizations are used. For small-sized FFT, our approach decomposes it into two radices, which can be fused later, while FFTW uses a kernel that utilizes only half the lanes of the SIMD register, resulting in significantly lower performance compared to GFFT. 5.3 Effectiveness of Optimization Techniques

Fig. 14 illustrates the improvement of each optimization in GFFT. The utilization of SIMD extension (AVX2) significantly boosts performance by 2.67x at maximum. The constant folding optimization further enhances performance with problem sizes larger than 2048, where twiddle operations could share twiddle factors. The operator fusion optimizations are effective for problems that fit in the level-2 cache, and our bi-directional recursive FFT algorithm is enabled and delivers a substantial performance boost for problem sizes greater than 2048.

6 CONCLUSION

Overall, GFFT is a highly optimized FFT framework that offers superior performance and flexibility on various processors. Its optimizations, such as the bi-directional recursive FFT algorithm and several graph optimizers, make it particularly well-suited for multi-core processors with SIMD extensions. Meanwhile, the tuning model we proposed proves efficient in FFT decomposition and execution. Furthermore, our task graph design supports mapping onto other hardware, which further increases its versatility, and makes it a valuable tool for various scientific and engineering applications that require efficient FFT computations. After evaluating the performance of GFFT on various processors, we conclude that GFFT outperforms other widely used FFT libraries such as FFTW and oneMKL in most test cases.

To enhance the versatility of the GFFT framework, future work will concentrate on high-performance FFT computing across multiple types of devices, including GPU and other hardwares. Additionally, we aim to implement capabilities similar to FFTW-MPI, which can leverage the computing power of large-scale supercomputers.
